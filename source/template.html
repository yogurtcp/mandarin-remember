Your routine

  1. Come back most days for about 10 minutes. Start with due reviews. If life interrupts, resume; there is no broken-streak penalty.
  2. Try before revealing. Say the Mandarin aloud or recall the meaning of what you hear. Give yourself about 5–10 seconds; if blank, check the answer rather than guessing endlessly.
  3. Compare, then rate honestly. A hint or a missing key word/tone means “needed help.” “Got it” means you recalled it without help. Other natural wording can be valid; compare meaning, not exact English wording.
  4. Repair a miss. Listen, say the model once, and add a short cue if useful. Let the app bring it back after a gap. Immediate familiarity is not proof that it will stick tomorrow.
  5. Begin with five new cards a day. This is a manageable starting point, not a scientifically optimal number. Choose 0 or 3 when reviews pile up. Finish in a few short sessions if needed.
  6. Use one item beyond the card. Say it in a real situation, or make a sentence about your day. Weekly, ask a Mandarin speaker for feedback on a short sample.
  7. Back up weekly. Your browser keeps your data locally; export/import moves it between devices.

What the evidence supports

Repeated retrieval and study spread across days improve delayed retention. Research on foreign-language vocabulary shows why continuing to retrieve matters after the first successful answer. The scheduler below applies these principles with a simple heuristic; it is not a validated guarantee of your personal memory.

  • Karpicke & Roediger, 2008: retrieval and foreign-language vocabulary
  • Dunlosky et al., 2013: practice testing and distributed practice
  • Cepeda et al., 2008: spacing depends on the retention goal

What your ratings mean

Production and understanding have separate due dates. New cards begin with a short retrieval step. Successful unassisted recall moves them to later days; misses bring them back sooner. A same-session retry never counts as delayed retention. Exact gaps, the 20-hour measurement threshold and daily limits are practical choices.

Mandarin pronunciation

Pinyin usually keeps underlying third tones: nǐ hǎo is normally pronounced with a rising first syllable. This deck shows common spoken changes of 一/不: yí ge, yì běn, bú yào. Unmarked syllables are light/neutral. Keep phrases together when listening. The app does not record your microphone or certify your tones.
